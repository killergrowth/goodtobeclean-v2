## What Is the Soda Blasting Process — and Why Does It Matter?

If you've heard about soda blasting but aren't sure what it actually involves, you're not alone. It sounds simple enough — baking soda, pressure, clean surface — but there's a lot more happening between that first phone call and the finished result. Whether you're dealing with grease-coated equipment, fire-damaged surfaces, or mold on wood, understanding the process helps you know what you're paying for and what to expect on job day.

Here's how we do it at Good To Be Clean in Wichita, KS — step by step.

---

## Step 1: Initial Assessment and Surface Evaluation

Every soda blasting job starts with a site evaluation. Before we touch anything, we look at:

- **What material is being cleaned** — wood, brick, metal, concrete, fiberglass, and painted surfaces all respond differently
- **What's on the surface** — grease, mold, char, paint, rust, or general grime each require different media pressure and distance settings
- **The surrounding environment** — nearby windows, landscaping, vehicles, and equipment that need to be protected

This step matters a lot. Soda blasting is gentler than sandblasting, but improper pressure on the wrong surface can still cause damage. Getting this right upfront is what separates professional results from a costly mistake.

---

## Step 2: Site Prep and Protection

Once we know what we're working with, we prep the area. That typically means:

- Masking off windows, doors, and adjacent surfaces
- Laying down containment barriers to capture spent media and debris
- Disconnecting or protecting nearby electrical components or sensitive equipment

Baking soda media is water-soluble and non-toxic, which makes cleanup much easier than traditional abrasive blasting — but we still treat every job site like it matters, because it does.

---

## Step 3: Soda Blasting Application

This is the core of the [soda blasting process](/service/soda-blasting/). Compressed air propels sodium bicarbonate (baking soda) particles at the surface. When those particles hit, they expand on impact — cleaning without gouging or abrading the substrate underneath.

Key variables our technicians control during this phase:

- **Pressure (PSI)** — lower for delicate wood or fiberglass, higher for stubborn grease or char
- **Nozzle distance** — closer for concentrated cleaning, further for broader coverage
- **Media flow rate** — adjusted based on buildup and surface type

The result is a surface that's clean at the microscopic level without the profile damage you'd get from sand or other aggressive media. That's why soda blasting is often the go-to choice for [fire and smoke restoration](/service/fire-and-smoke-restoration/) — it pulls char and soot out of wood grain without destroying the structure underneath.

---

## Step 4: Residue Rinse and Cleanup

Spent baking soda doesn't just disappear — it needs to be rinsed or swept depending on the environment. For most exterior surfaces, a water rinse is used to neutralize and remove the spent media. For indoor jobs or moisture-sensitive areas, dry containment and vacuuming may be used instead.

One of the real advantages here is that baking soda is non-hazardous. There's no chemical residue requiring special disposal, which keeps the process straightforward and safe — especially indoors or near landscaping.

---

## Step 5: Final Walkthrough and Quality Check

Before we call it done, we walk the entire job with you. We want you to see the results up close and ask questions. If anything needs a second pass or a touch-up, we handle it on the spot. Our goal is that you walk away confident the surface is clean — not that we just got it "good enough."
